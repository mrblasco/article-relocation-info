Additional comments from the Editors and Reviewers (if any):


Associate Editor: One reviewer is fully satisfied whereas the other has lingering concerns. I would like to see also the remainder of Reviewer 2's comments addressed.


Reviewer #1: The authors have addressed my concerns, which were minor anyway, in a satisfactory way. I congratulate them on a fine paper.


Reviewer #2: I thank the authors for a thorough and constructive response to the original review. The revised manuscript addresses most of my comments substantively, and I have no major outstanding concerns about the paper's core contribution or empirical approach.

I'd ask the authors to (a) briefly note the political-behavior link as future work or explain its omission, (b) rephrase the self-serving-bias vs. capacity-beliefs discussion to more evenly acknowledge that the two explanations are not fully distinguishable with the current design, and (c) add a simple country-level check of the salience/dose-response story behind the framing asymmetry. Once these edits are made, I would be glad to see the paper accepted.

Points well addressed:

- The new Discussion paragraph (p. 23) clarifies that GDP/population proxy for "capacity" in a specific, contestable sense, and that the fairness-ranking question may conflate mechanism-fairness with outcome-fairness. This is a fair
and transparent treatment of the ambiguity I raised.

- The added theoretical framing around Equity Theory (Adams 1965) in the Introduction gives the paper a clearer conceptual anchor for why individual fairness perceptions matter for policy.

- The discussion of the cross-country asymmetry in absolute vs. relative framing effects (p. 21) offers a plausible, well-motivated account grounded in denominator neglect and salience.

- The authors appropriately caveat the political-orientation heterogeneity results given the non-harmonized nature of left-right self-placement across countries, and — usefully — report that controlling for available (if crude) media
exposure measures leaves the results unchanged.

- The new heterogeneity analysis by perceived economic contribution of migrants (Section 4.4.6) is a welcome addition responsive to the spirit of my comments about unmodeled respondent beliefs.

Points to be strengthened:

- Mechanism linking preferences to policy behavior. The added theoretical framework is helpful, but my request to connect fairness perceptions to some observable political outcome (e.g., stated vote intention, party preference, or at least discussion of how these link empirically in other datasets) was not taken up. I don't consider this essential for publication, but I'd ask the authors to either add a sentence acknowledging this as an explicit avenue for future work, or explain briefly why such a link is outside scope.

- Self-serving bias vs. capacity beliefs. The manuscript still relies on an assertion that self-serving bias is the "most parsimonious" explanation for cross-country fairness differences, without a test that helps adjudicate between it and legitimate capacity concerns. Given the authors did not collect a direct capacity-perception measure, I accept this may not be resolvable with the current data — but I'd ask that the limitations paragraph be sharpened to state plainly that the two mechanisms are observationally difficult to separate here, rather than asserting one is more likely.

- Testing the salience explanation for the framing asymmetry. The authors offer a plausible narrative account for why the absolute-vs-relative framing effect concentrates in net-receiver countries, but this remains an ex-post interpretation. Since the underlying data (implied change per country, framing effect per country) already exist, I'd ask for a simple country-level correlation or scatterplot showing whether the size of the implied reduction/increase predicts the size of the absolute-vs-relative gap, to give this explanation some empirical teeth rather than resting on illustrative examples.

Note: Neither of these affects my overall assessment of the paper's validity or contribution, and I think all three concern interpretive scope and additional robustness rather than the design or execution of the experiment, which remains rigorous.
